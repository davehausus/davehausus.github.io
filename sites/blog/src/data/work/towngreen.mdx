## Overview

Town Green was a planned US $180 million mixed-use development in Scotts Valley, California, combining housing, retail, restaurants, public art, and green spaces. Before the development was built, the team needed a way to introduce the project to the community and keep interested residents informed as plans progressed.

I was responsible for designing and building the website from scratch, with a two-week deadline ahead of the project's first community event. The immediate priority was to establish a public-facing presence, communicate the project's vision, and give visitors a straightforward way to register their interest.

## Starting with limited resources

There was no existing website, established visual identity, or substantial collection of project assets to work with. The physical development was also still in the planning stages, so photographs of the site itself offered little to communicate what the finished place might be like.

The deadline meant I had to make decisions around what the website needed to accomplish at launch rather than attempt to represent every aspect of a project that was still evolving.

Two priorities shaped the work: communicate the intended character of Town Green without relying on finished-project photography, and make it easy for visitors to stay connected with the development team.

## Communicating a place that didn't exist yet

Instead of using photographs of the undeveloped land as the main visual reference, I selected images of plazas, green spaces, and active public areas from around California.

These images helped establish a visual direction for the proposed development. They gave visitors a reference for the kinds of spaces Town Green aimed to bring together, while avoiding the impression that the project was already built.

The imagery communicated an aspiration, not a representation of the finished development. That distinction mattered because the website needed to present a future place without implying that its buildings or public spaces already existed.

## Designing for mobile first

I started with the mobile layout and expanded it for desktop once the main structure was in place. This approach helped me establish the content hierarchy and primary actions before accounting for wider screens.

The layout focused on a few essential tasks:

- **Introduce the project.** A hero section presented the development and gave visitors a direct path to subscribe.
- **Keep the community informed.** An events section provided a place to list planned and past community events.
- **Offer another way to participate.** An opportunities and involvement section gave people a way to express interest or stay informed even if they couldn't attend an event.
- **Collect contact information.** A subscription form with minimal required fields let visitors register their interest without completing a lengthy form.

Each section supported the website's immediate purpose: helping people understand the project and giving them a way to maintain contact with the team.

## Building for the launch deadline

With two weeks to deliver the site, I handled both design and development. There was little value in building a complicated platform before the team had enough content or a more established identity to support it.

I concentrated on the essential page structure, responsive layouts, subscription and contact functionality, and the practical work of managing community sign-ups. This produced a working public website for the first community event, while leaving room for the team to add information as the project developed.

My responsibilities continued beyond the initial launch. I also managed and updated the subscriber database and community sign-ups, supporting the communication process the website was created to enable.

## Results

Between August 2018 and February 2019, approximately six months after launch, the website recorded:

- **1,915** unique visitors
- **3,617** page views
- **181** email subscribers
- **125** survey responses

These figures show that the website established an online audience and collected contact information and survey responses during its first six months. They document the activity generated around the project, although they don't establish how much the design itself contributed to that activity.

## What this project reinforced

The main constraint wasn't a lack of design possibilities. It was the gap between what the project intended to become and what could be shown at that stage.

Using reference imagery gave the website a way to communicate the intended character of Town Green before the physical development existed. A focused content structure then connected that introduction to practical next steps: finding event information, expressing interest, and subscribing for updates.

The project also reinforced the value of keeping the initial scope tied to the immediate need. A complete visual identity and an extensive set of features weren't prerequisites for launching a useful website. The team needed a clear starting point for communicating with the community, and the site provided one within the available timeframe.
